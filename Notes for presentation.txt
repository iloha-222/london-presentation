Detailed speaking notes for TseringSLIDE 1: THE THREE SACREDSGood morning everyone. Thank you for inviting me to speak with you today. I want to start by sharing something that my father, Tarthang Tulku Rinpoche, has always emphasized about Buddhist sacred art. He talks about what I think of as "the three sacreds": the sacred object, the sacred person, and the sacred place. These three aren't separate things in Tibetan Buddhist tradition, they're deeply interconnected.When you walk into a temple or stand before an altar, you're experiencing more than art on a wall or a statue on a shelf. The sacred object (a thangka painting, a statue, or a ritual implement) exists in relationship with the sacred person (the practitioner or community who engages with it). Both exist within the sacred place (the temple, the altar, the stupa, the monastery). These three create a living ecosystem of meaning and practice. The Tibetan Buddhist tradition has an unusually large output of sacred art. This isn't because we like decoration. It's because the Dharma has always been transmitted visually. Images are vehicles for study, for contemplation, for realization. When a young monk learns about Avalokiteshvara, the embodiment of compassion, he's not just reading about compassion as an abstract concept. He's studying the iconography: the thousand arms reaching out to help beings, the eleven heads representing different levels of awareness, the specific hand gestures and implements. The image teaches in ways that words cannot.But here's what concerns me, and what brings me here today. When these objects are removed from their context, when they're taken from altars, from temples, from communities, there's a rupture. The sacred objects are separated from sacred places and sacred persons. The thangka that once hung in a monastery where monks gathered daily for practice is now behind glass in a museum gallery. The statue that was the focus of generations of devotion is now an artifact in climate-controlled storage. The ritual implements that were held, used, empowered through ceremony are now specimens in a research collection. There's a violence in that separation, even if it happened long ago, even if it was done with the best intentions. The connection between object, person, and place is severed.And my question today is: is there a way to bring them back into relationship? Not necessarily physically, not requiring that every object return to its place of origin, because that's often not possible or even desirable given the political complexity of Tibet. But can we create new kinds of connections? Can we think creatively about how sacred objects in museums can reconnect with sacred persons and sacred places in ways that honor both the museum's role as caretaker and the living tradition's need for access?That's what I'm here to propose: a practical pathway forward that we could implement right now, while those larger questions are still being worked out. A way to make museum collections serve living education, living practice, living communities.SLIDE 2: A QUESTION BEYOND BORDERS: THE LUNAR MISSION STORYBefore I get into the specifics of what I'm proposing, I want to share something that happened earlier this year that really changed how I think about all of this. In March 2025, SINI participated in an extraordinary project called LunARC. This is an organization founded by my friend and colleague Lakshmi Karan. Lakshmi had this vision of sending community art from marginalized and indigenous peoples around the world to the moon. In collaboration with NASA and SpaceX, they carried a hard drive containing thousands of pieces of art created by communities that have traditionally been excluded from narratives about space exploration and humanity's future. This was explicitly about saying: space belongs to everyone, the future belongs to everyone, not just wealthy nations, not just space agencies, not just Silicon Valley billionaires.We submitted two pieces. One was a sacred image of Guru Rinpoche painted by Kaveri Singh, one of our artists. The other was a drawing by our principal, Ngawang Tsondu, of a young Tibetan girl. And on March 14th, 2025, these images landed on the moon. They're preserved digitally on a nano-chip that will last for a billion years.There's a term that astronauts use called the "overview effect." When you go to space and you see the Earth from far away, something shifts in your consciousness. From up there, you can't see the borders between countries. You just see this fragile, beautiful blue planet, and you understand in your bones that we're all connected, that we share one home, one interconnected ecosystem. Astronauts talk about how this experience creates a profound sense of oneness, of shared destiny.The LunARC mission was trying to create that same feeling here on Earth. If we can think about preserving art for a billion years on the moon, if we can think about these objects as belonging to humanity rather than to any single nation or ethnicity, doesn't that change the conversation we're having about museum collections?I recognize there's an enormous debate happening about restitution, about provenance, about colonial collections. Those are genuine tensions that need to be worked out through international dialogue, legal frameworks and community consultation.But I am here to say: while that debate continues, we could be doing something practical right now. We could be expanding the question. Not replacing "who owns this?" but adding another question alongside it: "How can this serve everyone? How can these collections become resources for living communities while also fulfilling museums' educational missions?"SLIDE 4: THE GAP Traditional Tibetan Buddhist monastic education is extraordinarily rigorous and sophisticated. A monk or nun studying to become a geshe, which is sort of like a PhD in Buddhist philosophy, will study for 15 to 25 years. They master logic, epistemology, metaphysics, ethics. They study the major texts of Indian and Tibetan Buddhism. They engage in intensive debate, training the mind to think precisely and to defend philosophical positions. They memorize vast amounts of material. They study ritual, meditation, tantric practices. It's a comprehensive, holistic education that has been refined over centuries. But here's what's generally not included: art history, material culture studies, conservation science, archival methods. These simply aren't part of the traditional curriculum.Meanwhile, museums hold extraordinary resources that could enrich Buddhist education: rare manuscripts that aren't available anywhere else, historical objects that document how practices evolved, research databases with detailed provenance information, conservation knowledge about how to preserve fragile materials. But these two worlds, traditional Buddhist education and museum scholarship, rarely connect. Monastics don't have access to museum collections in any systematic way. And when museums try to engage with Buddhist communities, they often struggle because there's no common language, no shared framework for understanding each other's priorities and practices.Let me give you a concrete example of why this matters. In museum collections, you'll find objects labeled something like: "Yab-Yum figure, Tibetan, 14th century, bronze with gilt and semi-precious stones." And there might be a description like: "This sculpture depicts a male and female deity in sexual union, representing the tantric practice of..."Now, this isn't wrong exactly. But it's a surface-level understanding. What's actually being represented in Yab-Yum imagery isn't primarily about gender or sexuality. It's about the inseparability of wisdom and compassion. And here's what's interesting: in the Tibetan tradition, wisdom is actually the feminine principle and compassion is the masculine principle. This reverses typical Western assumptions where rationality and wisdom are coded masculine and compassion and emotion are coded feminine.The imagery is teaching that genuine realization, enlightenment, requires both wisdom and compassion in perfect union. You can't have wisdom without compassion: that becomes cold, intellectual, disconnected from the suffering of beings. You can't have compassion without wisdom: that becomes overwhelmed, unable to discern how to actually help, sometimes even making things worse through misguided kindness. You need both qualities inseparably united.And this teaching is relevant not just for Buddhists. This is relevant for anyone thinking about how to live well, how to address the crises we face as a species. How do we cultivate both clear-seeing wisdom and heartfelt compassion? How do we bring these together in our actions? The image is teaching this in a way that words struggle to convey.This is what I mean by the knowledge gap. Museums have access to these objects but often lack deep understanding of what they're teaching. Buddhist communities have the traditional knowledge but lack access to the objects and to the scholarly resources museums have accumulated. Both sides could benefit from bridging this gap.SLIDE 4: WHY SINI CAN HELP BRIDGE THIS GAPLet me tell you about SINI and why I think we're particularly well-positioned to help build these bridges between museums and Buddhist communities.SINI was founded by my father, Tarthang Tulku Rinpoche, in Sarnath, India. For those of you who don't know, Sarnath is where the Buddha gave his first teaching after attaining enlightenment. It's one of the most sacred sites in all of Buddhism. Pilgrims come from around the world. Major teachings and gatherings happen there. So geographically, we're already in a significant location.My father left Tibet in 1959 after the Chinese invasion. Eventually he made his way to the United States, arriving in 1969 to teach and work for the preservation of Tibetan Buddhist culture. And for the past forty-plus years, that preservation work has taken many forms. In the 1960s, when he was teaching at Sanskrit University in Varanasi, he established one of the first printing presses to print sacred Tibetan texts. This was crucial work because so much was being lost: monasteries had been destroyed in Tibet, libraries had been burned, texts that had been copied by hand for generations were disappearing. So printing and preserving these texts was literally rescuing a tradition from destruction. In the United States, he founded the Tibetan Nyingma Meditation Center in Berkeley and established a publishing house, Dharma Publishing, that has produced hundreds of books in English and Tibetan. But there's one aspect of his work that's particularly relevant to what we're discussing today, and it's something that I think of as quietly rebellious. For decades, my father has been reproducing sacred art, particularly thangka paintings, from museum collections and distributing them back to monasteries and practitioners. Sometimes he gets permission from the museums to photograph and reproduce these images. Sometimes he doesn't. He just does it. He's printed and distributed thousands of these reproductions. He gives them to monasteries in India, Nepal, Bhutan. He distributes them at major teachings and gatherings. He makes them available to practitioners who want to study or who need images for their practice.So when I talk about partnerships between museums and Buddhist communities, I'm coming from this lineage. A lineage that says: yes, we understand museums have roles as caretakers, but we also need to be creative and ensure that sacred heritage serves living practice.Now, SINI itself started as a Nyingma institution. Nyingma is one of the four major schools of Tibetan Buddhism. But over time, we've become something different. We now have students from all four schools: Nyingma, Kagyu, Gelug, and Sakya. We have students from Tibet, from Bhutan, from Nepal, from India, from Mongolia. We've become this small hub, this place where different traditions can come together. And I think that's actually our unique value in this conversation about museums and heritage: we represent diversity on a small scale. We're small enough to be nimble, to try new things, but we're also connected to these much larger networks of Buddhist communities across Asia and in exile.We can be the connector between diverse Buddhist communities and European institutions. We can help smaller, more remote monasteries who don't have international connections, who don't have English-language capacity, who don't have anyone who understands how to navigate Western institutions. Because that's a reality: there are hundreds of small monasteries scattered across the Himalayan regions in remote places. They don't have advocates in the international arena, they don't have people who can engage in sophisticated discussions about museum ethics and conservation science and digital repatriation. This is where SINI can help. We can serve as a platform, a kind of stepping stone. We can help bring people to a place where they genuinely understand what's at stake. We can provide the language training, the cultural orientation, the educational foundation that makes real engagement possible. We can help create that level playing field so that when we talk about community consultation, it's genuine consultation.SLIDES 5-6: PRACTICAL PARTNERSHIP MODELS: MAKING THE THREE SACREDS WORK TOGETHERSo let me get specific about what I'm actually proposing. I think we need multi-layered educational partnerships that work on several levels simultaneously. Let me walk through each component.Digital AccessFirst and most basically, we need digital access to museum collections. The technology for this already exists and many museums have digitized significant portions of their collections. What I'm proposing is that museums intentionally make their Tibetan Buddhist collections accessible to monastic libraries and Buddhist educational institutions by actively partnering with institutions like SINI to ensure access.Imagine a young monk at SINI studying Vajrayana iconography. Right now, he's learning from his teacher, from texts, maybe from some printed images. But what if he could access high-resolution images from the British Museum's thangka collection? What if he could zoom in on details, compare different examples, see how iconography evolved across regions and time periods? This doesn't require objects to physically move and it doesn't require resolving debates about ownership. It just requires museums to digitize and share collections in partnership with educational communities.And the benefit isn't one-way: when Buddhist scholars and practitioners engage with digitized collections, they can provide feedback, corrections, additional information that enhances museum knowledge. They might identify objects that have been miscatalogued, provide information about ritual use that isn't in any published source or connect objects to specific lineages or practices. This becomes collaborative scholarship.The Fellowship ProgramThe second component, and really the heart of what I'm proposing, is a fellowship program. Each year, one or two graduates of SINI's English for Dharma Purposes program would spend three to six months embedded in a partner museum.Let me tell you a bit about our EDP program. EDP is a three-year intensive English language program specifically designed for Buddhist monastics. We currently have 28 students. We have six lopons, eight geshes, eight khenpos, and one khenmo. These are people who've completed anywhere from 15 to 25 years of traditional Buddhist education. They're experts in Buddhist philosophy, meditation, ritual practices.The challenge is that most of them come to English as adults. Many have limited background in secular academic studies. So we're not just teaching them English: we're preparing them to participate confidently in academic programs, to engage with Western institutions, to become bridges between traditional Buddhist scholarship and modern global discourse. And it's working. We've placed students at Oxford University and Harvard Divinity School. We have graduates who are now teaching Dharma in Germany, Amsterdam, the United States, Argentina, and Brazil. When I think about museum partnerships, these are the people I'm imagining as fellows. Imagine a geshe who's studied Buddhist philosophy and iconography for twenty years spending six months at the British Museum. He could study the collections, learn conservation techniques, contribute his knowledge to the museum's understanding of what these objects mean and how they were used. He could also teach and give public lectures bringing Buddhist perspectives to museum audiences. He could help with exhibition development, ensuring Buddhist content is presented accurately and respectfully. He could create educational resources: maybe video content explaining iconography, maybe written materials on ritual use, maybe curriculum guides that could be used by other Buddhist educational institutions.This is two-way learning. The fellow gains museum expertise, conservation knowledge, research skills, international experience, connections that last beyond the fellowship. The museum gains cultural expertise, Buddhist scholarly perspectives, community connections, innovative programming that brings new audiences.The educational resources created by fellows would flow back to SINI and to the broader network of Buddhist institutions we're connected with. So one fellow's work might impact hundreds of students over time. The knowledge gets multiplied.Sacred Sites IntegrationThe third component is something I'm really excited about because it brings us back to the three sacreds (object, person, place). With appropriate technology, we could display high-quality images of sacred art at sacred sites during major gatherings.Remember, SINI is located in Sarnath, one of the most sacred Buddhist sites. Bodgaya, where the Buddha attained enlightenment, is relatively nearby. Major teachings happen at these locations. Monlams (which are large prayer gatherings) can draw thousands of monastics.What if during a major teaching on, say, the Medicine Buddha, we could project high-quality images from museum collections related to that practice? Not random images, but pieces specifically relevant to that teaching, that lineage, that practice. With proper protocols, with appropriate framing, those digital presentations could help reconnect sacred objects with sacred places and sacred persons.This isn't meant to replace physical objects or to suggest that digital is equivalent to encountering the actual thangka or statue. But it's acknowledging that in our digital age, there are new ways to create meaningful connection. For many practitioners, seeing a high-quality image of a sacred object in the context of a teaching at a sacred site, where they can engage with it through practice, where it's connected to living transmission, might actually be more meaningful than seeing the physical object behind glass in a museum gallery thousands of miles away.This addresses something I talked about earlier, the rupture of the three sacreds. We're finding a way to bring them back into relationship, even if not in the original physical configuration.Cultural ProtocolsThe fourth component is developing cultural protocols collaboratively. I learned from the Swayambhu stupa renovation that details matter enormously. Let me tell you about that project because it's relevant here.In 2008, my father asked me to direct the renovation of Swayambhunath Stupa in Nepal. This is one of the most ancient and sacred Buddhist monuments in the world, a UNESCO World Heritage site. It's been renovated roughly every hundred years for the past thousand years. This was the fifteenth major renovation.I was 31 years old. I'm not a lama, not an architect, not an artist, not a contractor. I was a young woman, a foreigner, coming into a very traditional community and saying I'm going to direct this massive renovation. As you can imagine, there was enormous opposition.What I learned is that transparency and respect for cultural protocols are absolutely essential. We created a documentation process where we photographed, measured, and documented over 30,000 individual pieces of the stupa. Not a single nail was removed without being photographed with witnesses from local communities. Every piece was tracked through multiple processes.We also had to respect sacred protocols. Before we could begin the physical work, we had to do de-consecration ceremonies: complex rituals that temporarily extract the sacred essence of the stupa so that it can be worked on. These ceremonies were performed by Newari priests and Tibetan lamas over multiple days. When the renovation was complete, we had re-consecration ceremonies that His Holiness the Gyalwang Drukpa presided over, along with lamas from all the different schools. Throughout the project, we had to be mindful of proper orientation, proper handling, proper sequences. You don't put a Buddha statue upside down, you don't work on certain elements without appropriate blessings, etc. Now, museums are incredibly careful about handling objects. They use gloves, they control temperature and humidity, they document everything, they're meticulous about physical conservation. But the care is usually about the physical object, not the sacred significance.What if museums and Buddhist communities developed handling and display protocols together? What if decisions about orientation, about which objects could be photographed by visitors, about what's appropriate to have on public display versus in restricted access, what if these decisions were made collaboratively?This isn't about museums bowing to religious demands but about acknowledging that these objects have meanings and significance that go beyond their physical properties, and that respecting those meanings while maintaining rigorous conservation standards is actually possible. We proved this at Swayambhu: you can honor traditional sacred practices while also meeting international conservation standards. You can have both scientific rigor and spiritual respect.From the museum side, engaging with communities on protocols builds trust. It demonstrates that you see communities as partners, not as adversaries or as recipients of charity. It shows you take sacred significance seriously. From the community side, learning about conservation science, understanding why certain handling procedures exist, why climate control matters, why certain materials degrade and need special care, builds appreciation for the work museums do. It helps communities understand that museums aren't just hoarding objects but are engaged in complex preservation work.SLIDE 7: WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE FIVE YEARS FROM NOWLet me paint you a picture of what success could look like five years from now if we pursue these partnerships.Young monastics at SINI are studying museum collections as part of their regular curriculum.
