SLIDE 1: WHERE DO I SPEAK FROMI want to begin by sharing a bit about my background and why I'm approaching this conversation from the perspective I am. I come to this work through art and cultural preservation, not politics. My father, Tarthang Tulku Rinpoche, and I have dedicated our lives to Buddhist cultural preservation.In 2008, I directed the renovation of Swayambhu Stupa in Nepal, a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of the world's most ancient Buddhist monuments. This was the fifteenth renovation in 1,500 years, and I was the first woman to lead such a project.Over two years, we worked with more than 70 traditional artisans, renovated over 30,000 individual pieces using 23 kilos of gold, and created meticulous documentation: 60,000 images and 2,500 pages of reports. Not a single nail was removed without being photographed with community witnesses.What I learned through that project was how essential museums and archives are for this kind of preservation work. We were able to complete the renovation based on archival photos that we found in Nepal and at museums. We looked at their documentation of the art and architectural details, and based on that evidence, we recreated elements that had been lost or damaged. The museum resources gave us tangible guidance that the community could engage with meaningfully.This experience showed me the incredible value that museum collections can offer to living communities when there's a bridge connecting them. That's what I want to explore today: how we can build those bridges in ways that serve monastic education.SLIDE 2: THE THREE SACREDSI want to start by sharing something that my father, Tarthang Tulku Rinpoche, has always emphasized about Buddhist sacred art. He talks about what I think of as "the three sacreds": the sacred object, the sacred person, and the sacred place. These three aren't separate things in Tibetan Buddhist tradition—they're deeply interconnected.When you walk into a temple or stand before an altar, you're experiencing more than art on a wall or a statue on a shelf. The sacred object (a thangka painting, a statue, or a ritual implement) exists in relationship with the sacred person (the practitioner or community who engages with it). Both exist within the sacred place (the temple, the altar, the monastery). These three create a living ecosystem of meaning and practice.The Tibetan Buddhist tradition has an unusually large output of sacred art. This is because the Dharma has always been transmitted visually. Images are vehicles for study, for contemplation, for realization. When a young monk learns about Avalokiteshvara, the embodiment of compassion, he's not just reading about compassion as an abstract concept. He's studying iconography: the thousand arms reaching out to help beings, the eleven heads representing different levels of awareness, the specific hand gestures and implements. The image teaches in ways that words cannot.I recognize there's an enormous debate about restitution and responsibility. There are genuine tensions that need to be worked out through international dialogue, legal frameworks, and community consultation. But let me be clear about something: even if these objects were returned to their communities, that alone wouldn't be enough. It wouldn't be meaningful unless there was a deep educational component built into it. Otherwise, it's just another location. In India, there are many museums, but very few people visit them. Their educational resources are often quite poor.So today I want to talk about a practical pathway to make museum collections serve living education and living practice in Buddhist monastic communities. Something that can be practically implemented right now.SLIDE 3: THE OVERVIEW EFFECTBefore I get into the specifics, I want to share something that happened earlier this year that really changed how I think about cultural preservation. In March 2025, our Institute participated in an extraordinary project called LunARC, an organization founded by Lakshmi Karan. They carried a nano-chip containing thousands of pieces of art created by communities from areas that have experienced upheaval, and these were preserved on a nano-chip that will last millions of years. We submitted two pieces.As many of you may know, there's a term that astronauts use called the "overview effect." When you go to space and you see the Earth from far away, something shifts in your consciousness. From up there, you can't see the borders between countries. You just see this beautiful blue planet, and you understand that we share one home, one interconnected ecosystem, and that life is precious beyond words. Astronauts talk about how this experience creates a profound sense of oneness, of shared destiny.If we can think about preserving art for millions of years on the moon, if we can think about these objects as belonging to humanity rather than to any single nation, that might change the conversation about conservation.SLIDE 4: THE GAP & WHY SINITraditional Tibetan Buddhist monastic education is extraordinarily rigorous—a geshe studies for 15 to 25 years. But art history, material culture studies, and conservation science are generally not included. Meanwhile, museums hold extraordinary resources that these communities could benefit from, but these two worlds rarely connect.Let me tell you a bit about SINI and why I think we might be able to help build some bridges between museums and Buddhist communities.SINI was founded by my father in Sarnath, India, one of the most sacred sites in Buddhism, where the Buddha gave his first teaching. My father left Tibet in 1958 and established one of the first printing presses for Tibetan texts in the 1960s when so much was being lost. For decades, he's been reproducing sacred art, including from museum collections, and distributing them to monasteries and practitioners.Over time, we've had students from all four schools of Tibetan Buddhism, from Tibet, Bhutan, Nepal and India. There are hundreds of small monasteries scattered across the Himalayan regions. They don't have advocates, they don't have people who can engage in discussions about museum ethics and conservation science. Perhaps this is where SINI can help. Perhaps through our network, we could serve as a platform, a kind of stepping stone.SLIDE 5 : DIGITAL ACCESSLet me get specific about what I'm actually proposing. First and most practically, we need to know how to access existing digital collections. A series of trainings online for the Buddhist communities would be a great start. Once the monastic community knows what's available, the next step would be how to use these materials for their study.Our goal is to take some practical steps to go from a complete ignorance of the existence of these objects, to a passive experience of seeing it, to the next level where the material actually becomes useful and is transformed into an education that sustains the ecosystem from where these traditional sacred objects originally came from.Something being available, something being accessible, and something being used are three different things. You can have a museum collection, it can even be accessible, but if it's not used, if there's no training to help people engage with it, then the potential remains untapped.User ExperienceHere's something important to understand: when a geshe or monk at SINI thinks about these objects, they're not thinking "Pitt Rivers Museum" or "the Louvre." Those institutional names mean nothing to them. What's meaningful to them are the sacred sites—Sarnath, Bodh Gaya, Kushinagar, Lumbini—or they're thinking about lineage.This is where museum curators and technology meet in a very interesting way. In designing technology, we talk about user experience. If we're looking at a monastic student who's trying to engage with sacred arts and digital sacred arts, they're not thinking about whether something is located at a particular museum. They're thinking about whether this art comes from Sarnath, or from Lhasa, or is connected to specific teachings.Imagine if museum collections could be organized digitally in a way that relates to how practitioners actually think. What if there was a collective Sacred Art Bank that organized objects by sacred site and by lineage? All the Sarnath objects from museums around the world—from London, Paris, Frankfurt, New York—could be searchable together as "Sarnath Images." Similarly, all the Gelug lineage objects, or all the Nyingma lineage objects, could be grouped together.The collections aren't exactly hidden, but they're hard to navigate for someone who doesn't know the museum system. Even if everything is online and digitally available, the way to search for it can be hidden for the person who doesn't know how to use that system. We need to find ways to bridge that gap.SLIDE 6 : DIGITAL REPAIRSince I received this invitation for this program, I've been really thinking about how we can connect, and how the incredible resources of these museums could be useful for our students. I've been having conversations with students at SINI to understand their perspective on how they would actually use museum images.One thing that became very clear is how they would prefer images to be presented. Here's a concrete example: if there's an important statue in a museum collection, but it's broken or damaged, the way that it would be most useful for them would be to have it presented respectfully—not broken.They would be more likely to use an image that was more respectful and complete. This can be done relatively easily digitally. In the digital world, we could repair and restore images specifically for educational purposes. The museums preserve the art as it is, unchanged. But then, for educational use, we could digitally fill in the missing parts.The students also emphasized that they would love images to be lightened up, enhanced for clarity and visibility, so the details they need to study are clear and accessible. This kind of digital enhancement doesn't compromise the original; it creates teaching tools that serve living practice.SLIDE 7: THE SACRED ART EXCHANGETo build on this, we'd propose to have an exchange between SINI and museums. This is really about curriculum work through exchanges—museums working with our communities in meaningful educational partnerships.Graduates of our program could come and be hosted at museums to see firsthand the objects. And curators could come to SINI to share their knowledge and learn about the three sacreds.Let me give you a concrete example of how we've approached this kind of visual education at SINI. When we were doing text preservation work with the Kangyur Karchak, we did an incredible exercise. Students and translators were taking notes about particular Buddha teachings—a sutra might describe deer, squirrels, a purple lotus, mountains in a certain way, 500 monks. If that sutra didn't have a widely known illustration, we created images. Our artist Kaveri Singh hand-painted these stories from the Buddha's life, and we used those as illustrations in the Kangyur Karchak.We took the story that was written and transformed it into an image. It became such a powerful teaching tool: most people are visual learners. Many monastery classes don't even have projectors, so having visual images to support education becomes crucial. Museums could think about how their art pieces can serve as teaching aids in exactly this way.In the fellowship exchange, fellows could study collections, learn conservation techniques, contribute their knowledge, and create educational resources that flow back to SINI and to the broader network of Buddhist institutions. This is two-way learning that multiplies over time.SLIDE 8: WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKEFive years from now:Young monastics at SINI are studying museum collections as part of their regular curriculum.
